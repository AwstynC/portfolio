QAOA Final Report: Quantum vs Classical Max-Cut

QAOA
Quantum
Course Project
Published

April 23, 2026

Project Vision and Goals

This project set out to explore how quantum computing approaches the Max-Cut problem differently from classical methods. Using Google’s Cirq library, I implemented the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) and compared it against brute force and greedy classical baselines across graphs ranging from 4 to 30 nodes.

My six measurable goals were: formulating Max-Cut as an Ising Hamiltonian, implementing QAOA in Cirq, simulating quantum circuits, building classical baselines, evaluating approximation ratios, and analyzing runtime scaling.

Results

All six goals were achieved. Key findings from the scaling experiments:

n Brute Force Greedy Ratio QAOA Ratio BF Time QAOA Time
4 10.350 1.000 1.000 ~0.000s ~0.936s
10 45.770 0.839 0.959 0.001s ~2.054s
20 169.310 0.899 0.877 3.308s ~6.663s
25 255.040 0.844 0.894 157.235s ~81.803s
30 361.540 0.839 0.858 7058.681s ~6181.511s

QAOA consistently outperformed greedy on smaller graphs but both methods degraded at larger sizes, an expected consequence of p=1 circuit depth.

The most striking result was runtime scaling. At n=30, brute force took 118 minutes while QAOA simulation took ~103 minutes. Projecting forward, brute force would take ~43 years at n=50. On real quantum hardware, that same QAOA circuit would run in microseconds.

Reflection

The hardest part was finding research that explained expected results without giving away the implementation. Significant work constraints — four week-long field projects and a two-week remote dam maintenance assignment — made data collection difficult. The unexpected passing of my last close grandparent added further challenge during the project period.

The most surprising finding was QAOA occasionally losing to greedy. This turned out to be expected behavior at p=1 under classical simulation constraints and is not a flaw in the implementation.

What’s Next

Given more time I would run circuits on real quantum hardware via IBM Quantum, implement higher p values to show quality improvement with circuit depth, and benchmark against the Goemans-Williamson semidefinite programming approach.
